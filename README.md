# Equity Derivatives Pricing and Hedging Simulator

## Introduction

This project starts from the Black-Scholes model to build, step by step, a pricing and hedging simulator for equity options: pricing a European call/put, the Greeks (delta, gamma, vega, theta, rho), implied volatility, simulating the underlying, and delta hedging with analysis of the resulting P&L.

The goal isn't to reproduce a professional desk's tooling, but to understand in depth — and be able to concretely show — how these theoretical building blocks fit together: why an option seller who is delta-hedged can still lose money, how rebalancing frequency affects risk, and what changes when testing all this on real market data instead of simulated paths.

## Theory

### Black-Scholes

The model assumes the underlying follows a geometric Brownian motion (log-normal returns, constant volatility σ and risk-free rate r, no transaction costs, European exercise only). Under these assumptions, the price of a call and a put with continuous dividend q are given by:

```
C = S·e^(−qT)·N(d1) − K·e^(−rT)·N(d2)
P = K·e^(−rT)·N(−d2) − S·e^(−qT)·N(−d1)

d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r − q + σ²/2)·T] / (σ√T)
d2 = d1 − σ√T
```

These two prices aren't independent: **call-put parity** (`C − P = S·e^(−qT) − K·e^(−rT)`) must always hold, and serves as a basic consistency check in `tests/test_pricing.py`.

### Greeks

Each Greek measures the price's sensitivity to a variable: delta (spot), gamma (change in delta, i.e. convexity in spot), vega (volatility), theta (time), rho (rate). `src/greeks.py` implements them by differentiating the formulas above directly. The [Vega vs gamma](#vega-vs-gamma-whats-the-difference) section below details the difference between the two second-order sensitivities.

### Implied volatility and hedging

Implied volatility is the inverse operation: instead of starting from σ to compute a price, you start from an observed price and recover the σ that justifies it (numerical inversion of Black-Scholes, see `src/implied_volatility.py`). Delta hedging, meanwhile, uses delta as a hedge ratio: holding `Δ` shares per option sold (locally) neutralizes spot exposure. Since Δ changes with spot (because of gamma), the hedge has to be rebalanced — hence the residual P&L studied in the Results section.

#### Vega vs gamma: what's the difference?

Both measure a second-order sensitivity, but not with respect to the same variable:

- **Gamma** = sensitivity of delta to **spot**. Answers: *"if the underlying moves $1 right now, how much does my delta (hedge ratio) change?"* It materializes through actual moves in the underlying — that's why it's tied to delta hedging (it dictates the rebalancing speed).
- **Vega** = sensitivity of price to **volatility**. Answers: *"if the market changes its mind about future volatility, how much does the price change, even if spot doesn't move?"* It materializes even without any move in the underlying — just because the market reprices future risk (e.g. an earnings announcement is coming up, implied vol rises, the option gains value even though spot hasn't moved).

The two charts above illustrate this as mirror images: gamma is high when maturity is **short** (the price has to "make up its mind" fast), vega is high when maturity is **long** (more time = more potential uncertainty over the path of vol). A short-dated option is more of a "gamma" play, a long-dated one more of a "vega" play.

### Delta hedging simulation

`plot_hedge_simulation()`

A simulated path (K=100, T=1, r=5%, σ=20%, rebalanced at every step), seen from the perspective of a delta-hedged call seller. Three panels synced in time: spot, the rebalanced delta (which tracks spot closely and falls to 0 when the option ends up deep OTM), and mark-to-market P&L (hedge portfolio value minus the theoretical value of the option still owed). P&L moves in small jumps at each rebalance — that's the friction of discrete hedging (rebalancing continuously would be the theoretical ideal, impossible in practice).

![Delta hedging simulation: spot, delta and P&L over time](plots/hedge_simulation.png)

### Daily vs weekly rebalancing

`plot_hedge_simulation(rebalance_every=5)`

Same path, but rebalanced only every 5 steps (~weekly) instead of every day. The held delta (orange) tracks the target delta (grey) in a stair-step fashion, with a real gap between rebalances — especially visible around t=0.55 and t=0.9, where P&L makes sharper net jumps.

![Delta hedging simulation with weekly rebalancing](plots/hedge_simulation_weekly.png)

**Statistical comparison** (2000 paths, K=100, T=1, r=5%, realized vol = implied vol = 20%):

| Frequency | Mean P&L | P&L std dev | Avg. rebalances |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily (every step) | −0.006 | 0.436 | 253 |
| Weekly (every 5 steps) | −0.019 | 0.953 | 52 |

Mean P&L stays close to zero in both cases (the hedge isn't biased), but the **standard deviation nearly doubles** for weekly rebalancing at 5x fewer rebalances. This is the real trade-off of discrete delta hedging: rebalancing less often reduces transaction costs but sharply increases risk (P&L dispersion) — rebalancing continuously would be the theoretical ideal (zero risk), impossible in practice.

### Threshold-based (adaptive) rebalancing

`plot_hedge_simulation(rebalance_threshold=0.05)`

A third mode: instead of a fixed interval, rebalancing happens as soon as the theoretical delta drifts away from the held delta by more than a given threshold (5% here). The held delta tracks the target delta much more closely than in the weekly case — the threshold rebalances more when the market really moves, and less when it's calm.

![Delta hedging simulation with 5% threshold rebalancing](plots/hedge_simulation_threshold.png)

**Full comparison** (2000 paths, same parameters):

| Strategy | Mean P&L | P&L std dev | Avg. rebalances |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily | −0.013 | 0.431 | 253 |
| Weekly | −0.025 | 0.959 | 52 |
| 5% threshold | 0.006 | 0.678 | 38.7 |
| 10% threshold | 0.029 | 1.058 | 15.6 |

Notable result: at a comparable number of rebalances, the **threshold beats the fixed schedule**. The 5% threshold (38.7 rebalances, slightly fewer than weekly) achieves a standard deviation of 0.678, well below weekly's 0.959 (52 rebalances). Rebalancing *when the market moves* rather than *on a schedule* reduces risk for an equivalent transaction budget — which is what most desks do in practice.

### Realized dispersion: AAPL / MSFT / NVDA / AMZN / META basket

`plot_dispersion_analysis()`

**Note: "realized" dispersion, not an actual desk dispersion trade.** A real dispersion trade sells index *implied* vol and buys the constituents' *implied* vol — this project has no real options data (see the caveat on `plot_implied_vol_smile()` above). What follows only uses *realized* vol and correlation on real prices, to illustrate the underlying mechanism: why an index's vol is almost always lower than the average of its constituents, and the exact role correlation plays in that gap.

Five real stocks (`AAPL`, `MSFT`, `NVDA`, `AMZN`, `META`), equally weighted, prices over 2 years via `yfinance`. The "mini-index" is built by indexing each stock to 100 at the start and then summing the weighted levels — so that each stock's dollar price doesn't distort its weight.

**Round-trip check**: the basket's vol can be computed two independent ways.
- *Direct*: compute the returns of the basket itself, just like for any stock → **26.2%**.
- *Via the formula*: start only from the individual vols and the correlation matrix, and reconstruct `σ_basket² = wᵀ Σ w` (with `Σᵢⱼ = σᵢ σⱼ ρᵢⱼ`) → **25.8%**.

The two methods agree to within ~0.4 points (a small expected gap: the formula is exact for simple returns, but is applied here to log returns, whose weighted sum isn't rigorously equal to the basket's log return). This round-trip validates the correlation computation, exactly like the price → implied vol → price round-trip validated the Black-Scholes inversion.

Once the formula is validated, it isolates the correlation effect: the weighted average of the individual vols is **34.9%**, well above the basket's ~26%. The gap (~9 points) is entirely due to diversification — pairwise correlations (0.31 to 0.58 on the heatmap) are well below 1, so the stocks' moves partly cancel out instead of adding up. This is precisely the gap a dispersion trade tries to monetize: if the market prices in a higher implied correlation than the correlation that actually materializes, selling index vol and buying the constituents' vol is profitable (and vice versa).

![Correlation and diversification effect on a 5-stock basket](plots/dispersion_analysis.png)

## Limitations

This project is an educational tool, not a desk tool. Main limitations to keep in mind when reading the results above:

- **No real implied volatility data.** No source of real option prices is used: the smile in the Results section is entirely synthetic, and the "implied vol" used in delta hedging on real data is actually historical realized vol. A real dispersion trade is played on *implied* vols and correlation, not realized ones — this project can only illustrate the underlying mechanics (see the Dispersion section).
- **Black-Scholes itself is a simplification**: constant volatility and rate, no price jumps, European exercise only (no American options), no transaction costs or liquidity constraints in delta hedging.
- **Real data not cleaned to a professional level**: uses what `yfinance` provides as-is (holidays, splits, dividends handled by `auto_adjust=True`), with no additional processing.
- **The "mini-index" in the Dispersion section is a simplified construction** (an equally-weighted basket of 5 stocks indexed to 100), not a real index with float-market-cap weighting and a rebalancing methodology.

## Interactive interface (Streamlit)

**Live app: [equity-derivatives-simulator-wgrj8rugktksm3x3qh4jpz.streamlit.app](https://equity-derivatives-simulator-wgrj8rugktksm3x3qh4jpz.streamlit.app)** (free Streamlit Community Cloud hosting — the app may take a few seconds to wake up after a period of inactivity).

```

```

The app reuses the functions from `src/` directly (no duplicated logic) — the plotting functions in `src/plots.py` accept `save_path=None` to avoid writing anything to disk in interactive mode, and return the matplotlib figure instead of just displaying/saving it.
